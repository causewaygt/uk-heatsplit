1. What heat costs now — and what geothermal would change

What heat costs to make — by route, wholesale commodity

WHY THESE NUMBERS — what sets the price of each route, in plain terms

Why the heat pumps are not all in one place. A heat pump does not make heat, it moves it, and the cost is set by how far it has to move it. An air-source unit lifts from whatever the outside air is doing, which on the coldest mornings is the moment the house wants most heat - the efficiency falls exactly when the demand peaks. Ground source lifts from ground that sits near 10 °C all winter, so the gap it works across barely changes between October and February. A geothermal network starts warmer still and shares one set of boreholes and one loop across many buildings, so each building is lifting a short distance on a shared infrastructure.

Why hot water is the expensive half. Space heating can run at a low flow temperature on a mild day, and the heat pump rewards that. A hot-water cylinder wants its 52 °C whatever the weather, so none of that benefit is available - the lift is fixed and the efficiency with it. A gas boiler is indifferent: it burns the same gas for the cylinder as for the radiators. That is most of why the AS DELIVERED view is dearer than the SPACE HEATING one for every heat pump on the chart, and barely moves gas at all.

Why electricity carries more than its fuel. On the AT THE METER basis each route is priced at what a household actually pays, and the policy and network costs loaded onto a unit of electricity are several times those on a unit of gas. Switch to WHOLESALE and that loading comes off both: the heat pumps move down the chart, gas moves far less, and the distance between the two views is the part of the price that is a policy choice rather than a physical one.

Why gas looks dear today and cheap over the year. The gas routes are flat lines that step: they are the price cap, and it is reset quarterly. A mean taken over a long window still carries the months when the cap was lower, so the twelve-month figure sits below today's. The heat pumps have no such steps - their lines wander daily with the weather, because the weather is what sets the lift.

Why the means are weighted by heat. A plain average over days would count a mild September day, when almost no heat is delivered and every heat pump looks excellent, the same as a January day carrying twenty times the load. Weighting by the heat actually delivered puts the figure where the energy is - and it is what moves air-source above gas on the year rather than below it.

Why resistive electric is hidden. Direct electric heat is a unit of electricity for a unit of heat, with no lift and no multiplier, so it runs near 26p and the axis has to stretch to about three times its height to reach it. Everything the panel is about then happens in the bottom third. It is on the toggle because it is the ceiling every other route is measured against, not because it is a route anybody is choosing.

WHY THESE NUMBERS — what cooling data can and cannot tell you, in plain terms

Why nobody can just measure national cooling. Cooling is electricity, and electricity all looks the same on a meter. A chiller running in an office is indistinguishable from a kettle except in one respect: it responds to temperature. So the grid can show how cooling changes as the weather changes, and it can never show how much there is in total, because a load that runs flat all summer hides inside every other constant in the system. That is why the levels on this panel come from building surveys and national statistics, and the grid is used to test them rather than to produce them.

6. UK geothermal: now, next and benchmarks

What should our geothermal targets be?

CONTEXT — how this compares, and the targets published in 2026

—

Two targets, both published in October 2026. The National Geothermal Centre’s Our Geothermal Nation sets 10 GW of heat and 1.5 GW of power by 2050, illustrated as ~70 and ~11.8 TWh a year, from about 1 GW and 0.3% of UK heat today. That 70 TWh implies ~7,000 full-load hours against the 1,661 measured on the UK’s shallow fleet, so it describes a far deeper, district-heat-led build than the bars above†. EGEC’s declaration of 29 October 2026 calls for 250 GW across the EU by 2040 — over 1,000 TWh a year, a fifth of projected 2040 final energy demand excluding transport.

7. Value for money? An illustrative appraisal of geothermal against an air-source-led network

One counterfactual, on Green Book conventions — not a full appraisal

This does not ask whether to build the networks. Government has committed to heat networks supplying about 20% of UK heat by 2050, up from about 3% today, on more than £80bn of investment — roughly 87 TWh a year of delivered heat. The pipes, the trenches and the connections are in that number either way. What is still open is what sits at the head of them. The model compares geothermal as the source and store of thermal energy to an air-source heat pump system.

What this appraisal is, and is not. It is one counterfactual: geothermal against an air-source-led network with separate air-cooled chillers. That is a real and increasingly likely alternative, because zoning is designating networks without settling their source — but it is not the only one. A network can also be fed by industrial waste heat, river or sewer heat, data-centre rejection, large water-source heat pumps, energy from waste, or a hybrid of several. Nothing here establishes that geothermal is the preferred national source; it establishes how geothermal compares with one named alternative on Green Book conventions. It is also not a full Green Book appraisal. It applies Green Book conventions — the sixty-year horizon, the declining discount rate, DESNZ carbon values, optimism bias — to the increment between two sources. It does not carry a full options appraisal, a distributional analysis, or unmonetisable effects, all of which the Green Book also asks for. Added 17 August 2026 after peer review.

What is not in the numbers

Subsurface risk — the size is priced, the remedy is not
There is little or no UK experience of geothermal systems at this scale, and UK outturn data barely exists. Rather than leave that as an unquantified caveat, the subsurface shortfall lever puts it in the numbers as capacity the ground does not deliver — whether from a failed well, too little recharge, a store driven warm by over-reinjection, or a round-trip below design. What remains outside the numbers is which failure mode it is and when it appears, since each has a different fix and a different cost to correct. Two things keep it small: a balanced store, returning in summer what winter takes, and European practice transferred in at design, since the Dutch, Danish and French fleets carry decades of monitored operation.
Connection and take-up
Mandatory connection inside designated zones removes the risk that historically killed UK heat network cases. Outside zones it is unprotected, and no published UK benchmark of realised against forecast connection rates was found. It appears here as a switching value rather than a modelled cost.
The coincident-peak claim is model-derived
No published UK study compares the winter peak of two otherwise identical networks differing only in heat source. The mechanism is evidenced — monitored air-source efficiency falls to 2.44 on the coldest days against 3.24 for ground source — but the head-to-head figure comes from an hourly model of instantaneous performance, not a field trial.
The cooling increment has no UK primary source
The cost of making one loop deliver cooling as well as heat is set at £75 per kW, on a range of £0 to 150. No published UK study separates a heating-and-cooling shared loop from an otherwise identical heat-only one, so this is an engineering estimate rather than a benchmark, and it is the weakest number on the panel. The chiller rate it is netted against is now bounded by UK evidence at both ends — about £100 per kW for equipment alone and about £390 for a 2025 NHS installed contract — but that rate exists by size band only in the commercial cost books. A review of 133 aquifer storage systems, which are inherently heating and cooling, puts a whole ground system near £255 per kW above 2 MW: the same order as a heat-only base plus a small increment, which is corroboration rather than measurement. The sign of the difference would reverse only if cooling drove the borefield sizing, if the loop had to be upsized for cooling flow, or if building plant had to be replaced rather than specified as reversible from the start.

What would change on the strength of this

  1. Zoning designates where networks go. It does not appraise what feeds them. On these numbers that second decision carries a benefit-cost ratio above 2 on an increment of about a ninth of the programme's own capital.
  2. Require a source options appraisal at zone designation. Ground, aquifer and mine water against air source, on whole-life cost including avoided peak, not capital alone.
  3. Publish outturn against sanctioned cost for every funded scheme. Optimism bias here rests on a 2002 categorical table, not on a reference class of completed heat networks, because no UK dataset exists. The Green Heat Network Fund is building one scheme by scheme — publishing it would give the sector a real base rate within a few years. Flyvbjerg’s own finding argues it would be a favourable one: modular programmes, built the same way many times, are the best performers in a database of more than 16,000 projects, and a heat network rollout is modular where a tunnel is not.
  4. Map the resource against the zones. One property in four sits on Britain’s coalfield, which covers 11% of its land area (Mining Remediation Authority) — a quarter of the buildings on a ninth of the ground, and the zoned towns and cities are largely the same places.

Gas (live). Daily gas offtake to Britain's local distribution zones (LDZ — the network serving homes and most businesses) is regressed against population-weighted GB heating degree days (ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo). The temperature-sensitive component is attributed to space heating. Directly-connected power stations are excluded. Approach follows the published Watson/Sansom method. Current fit: —.

Everything else (shaped annual). Oil, electric heating, bioenergy, heat networks, solid fuel and cooling take their annual levels from the DESNZ ECUK 2025 End Use tables (calendar 2024) and are distributed across the year by heating/cooling degree days. They do not respond to live grid data. Cooling is the weakest element: the ECUK figure combines cooling and ventilation, split here 50% flat / 50% weather-shaped by assumption, and UK cooling demand is poorly measured. Electric heating counts the electricity into heat pumps, not the ambient heat they harvest — so the useful heat delivered by heat pumps is understated for now.

The two bars. The top bar is final energy — fuel and electricity purchased. The bottom bar is useful heat and cooling delivered: combustion derated by measured in-situ efficiencies (gas 83.5%, oil 82%, bio 70%, solid 55% — the last three are estimates), heat networks shown at delivered heat, resistive electric at 100%, heat pumps multiplied by a blended SPF of 2.8 (Electrification of Heat, 742 metered homes) with the harvested ambient heat shown in teal, and the weather-driven half of cooling multiplied by an assumed EER of 3. Heat-pump electricity is the ECUK 2025 domestic figure (2.0 TWh, 2024); non-domestic heat pumps are not yet counted, so the teal segment is understated.

Boiler efficiency, by end use. Space heating and hot water are converted at different efficiencies. A boiler serving only a cylinder fires in short bursts, cools between them, and returns water above the condensing dewpoint, losing its latent recovery: gas is 0.835 for space heating and 0.73 for hot water, oil 0.82 and 0.71. Both hot-water figures are the mean SAP 2012 Table 4b summer/winter ratio applied to the in-situ winter anchor, and SAP applies the summer figure to hot water in every month, not seasonally. Summer runs 82–89% of winter across that table. Bioenergy and solid fuel keep a single figure, because Table 4b does not cover them. The effect is that the effective gas efficiency now moves with the season — about 0.81 in January and 0.74 in July, when the load is almost all hot water.

Known biases. Hot-water heating is treated as flat but is mildly seasonal (colder mains water in winter), so gas space heating carries a small upward bias. Table 4b is SAP’s fallback for boilers absent from the Product Characteristics Database, so it is conservative and skewed to older plant; a PCDB-weighted fleet figure would beat it. Table 4c deducts 5 points where a regular boiler has no interlock, which is not applied here.

Geothermal panel. Today's heat figure is anchored on the EGEC 2025 UK Country Update (1,430 GWhth/yr from ~55,210 GSHP units, 847–861 MWth installed — a 2023 estimate from sales data; the Environment Agency's 2024 review suggests only ~30–38k of those units may be operational, hence the 1.4–2.0 range) plus ~0.07 TWh/yr from mine-water, deep and open-loop district schemes. No national metering or single register of ground-source systems exists; cooling is anchored only on 11 ATES systems (~8 MWth cold) and historic Southampton data. Forward bars are third-party pathways (CCC, Project InnerSpace/REA/ARUP) or explicitly flagged Causeway derivations, not predictions. The 2031 bar carries a range of roughly 3.5–6 TWh/yr. If the NI DfE licensing proposals (consultation to 7 Aug 2026; heat below 100 m depth) proceed, the resulting register with published system performance would be the UK's first mandatory geothermal data source — this panel is designed to ingest it from ~2027.

Northern Ireland, and the UK-to-GB step. NI is not on the GB network shown here. NI heating is estimated annually from subnational consumption statistics shaped by NI degree days, and that estimate now has a source: NIHE and BRE modelled NI domestic heat at 14.2 TWh (10,700 GWh primary space heating plus 3,500 GWh water) from the House Condition Survey, which is within 2% of the figure this site was already carrying. The annual figures behind every other series are UK totals from ECUK, scaled to GB per fuel. Until 17 August 2026 a single gas-network factor of 0.985 was applied to all of them, which is a statement about the GB gas grid and does not transfer. It was wrong for oil, where NI holds a far larger share than its population: NIHE put NI oil heat at 10.8 TWh on a 2016 basis, and the Irish sibling site estimates 8.3 TWh on a trailing twelve months — not a conflict but nine years apart, with NISRA showing the oil share of NI homes falling from 68% to 61% over that period. On the current basis NI holds about 17% of UK oil heat, so the shares are now per fuel: gas 0.988, oil 0.830, solid fuel 0.893, everything else the household share of 0.972†. NOTES — what is on the map, what is held back, and why 45 kW

Everything above this point is inferred from national data. This is the other kind of evidence — individual systems, located, each with a source. It is a locator, not a statistic: an opt-in register carries the same selection bias as any opt-in monitoring sample, so nothing here is averaged, and nothing here feeds the figures above.

—

Why 45 kW. It is the MCS ceiling (MIS 3005) and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme limit. Above it a system sits outside domestic certification and is designed bespoke — a defensible line between a building's system and a household's appliance. Domestic systems are not collected at all — not as points, not as counts, not as postcodes. A full postcode plus "has a ground-source heat pump" is one lookup from an address, because the EPC register is public, searchable by postcode, and records the main heating system, so the register does not ask. Nothing is lost by that: MCS registration already counts the domestic base as a census, which is a better figure than a self-selected one could be. The threshold is a privacy line that happens also to be a sensible engineering one.

Two sites in the table sit below the line and are on it anyway — a demonstration standing column at Markham and a social housing scheme at Lisnafin. Neither is a household's appliance; both were entered by hand for precedent. They are in the register and off the map.

Colour carries nothing. Green and orange are indistinguishable to a deutan reader on this background, so identity is shape and size, which also survive greyscale and print.

Geothermal archetypes

The site models heat and storage, not power. The two columns inside the green boundary — shallow heating, cooling and storage, and intermediate heating and storage — are in scope. The deep, high-temperature systems on the right are not, because their output is electricity and this site is about what heats and cools buildings. The two modelled routes map onto the green columns and not onto the orange one.

Ground source (SPF 3.24) is the shallow column on the left — open-loop doublets, closed-loop borehole arrays and mine water energy, drawing on ground and shallow aquifers at 10–17 °C. It is modelled here as heat only or heat dominated: a source temperature of 8 °C after a 3 K brine approach, weather-compensated flow, and no credit for anything the ground gets back. That is deliberately the conservative end of what shallow geothermal can do, and the seasonal performance factor comes from In-Situ Heat Pump Performance: Analysis of Ofgem Data 2017–22 — a DESNZ, RECC and Ground Source Heat Pump Association study of Ofgem metering returns. Its 1,700+ installations cover both technologies; the ground-source sample is roughly 270. No large, recent, representative UK ground-source fleet exists: the three available samples are about 270, 37 and 14 installations, so 3.24 is the best available figure rather than a well-evidenced one†.

Future Geothermal (SPF* 5.0†) is a blend of two things in the figure. Shallow underground thermal energy storage on the left, where summer heat rejected by cooling and waste heat from data centres and other sources is banked in the ground and drawn back in winter; and intermediate geothermal — the hydrothermal direct-use column — where the geology suits it. Both feed a shared ambient loop with heat pumps at each building.

Why SPF* and not SPF. The other routes carry a measured seasonal performance factor from a monitored fleet. This one does not, and no UK fleet exists to give it one. SPF* is an assumed blend across geothermal archetypes — shallow storage and intermediate direct use — expressed as delivered heat over electricity bought, so it spans a route where a heat pump does the lifting and one where a doublet largely does not. It is wired into the model the same way the others are: an assumed average source temperature of 19.6 °C and the same Carnot fraction the two monitored routes calibrate to. The asterisk marks it as derived from those two assumptions rather than observed, and the two are one estimate with two faces — fix either and the other follows†.

The difference between the two routes is mostly what the loop is sitting at. A network loop is modelled at 19.6 °C†, not the 8 °C of a passive ground array, because it is a charged loop: the cooling season and the rejected waste heat have put energy into it. That shortens the lift, which is what a heat pump’s efficiency actually depends on.

This is the least evidenced route on the site. There is no monitored UK network fleet to anchor an SPF against, so both the 5.0 and the 19.6 °C are Causeway estimates, and every network figure inherits them.

Both routes are first named in Section 3, “How far would electrified heat have sat against national capacity?”, and appear again in Section 2’s price chart and Section 6’s value case.
